\chapter{Results}

The central focus of this project was to develop a pipeline to identify structured RNA elements and to apply this in the search of candidate light-responsive RNA elements (or ``light switches'').
This was done by developing a computational discovery pipeline and testing it on a positive control to rediscover the \ac{FMN} riboswitch, and against a set of negative controls.
Further, a set of novel approaches to comparing covariance models was developed and tested on a curated set of Rfam families, and then applied to the positive control candidates to assess their similarity to the \ac{FMN} riboswitch.

\section{Positive control search: Can protein homology be rediscovered from known \ac{RNP}s}

% go through fmn riboswitch, other riboswitches and crispr examples briefly
The hypothesis on which this work is based is that protein homology can be leveraged to identify RNA homology in \ac{RNP} or cisregulatory systems, on the basis that they are encoded at neighbouring loci.
Approaching the hypothesis from the other direction, the positive control pipeline described in Figure~\ref{fig:poscontrol} searches for protein homology in coding sequences neighbouring known RNA family members.
Clustering of neighbouring protein sequences, by pairwise alignment, was used as a proxy for homology.

The \ac{FMN} riboswitch, \ac{SAM} riboswitch, \ac{CRISPR}-DR42 and Fluoride riboswitch were all found to be associated with particular conserved protein domains, RibD C-terminal domain, Cys/Met metabolism PLP-dependent enzyme, CRISPR-associated protein Cas2 and CrcB-like protein camphor resistance, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:results_summary}).
These protein domains were identified in the highest prevalence cluster of coding sequences neighbouring the RNA family members, and the position of the RNA element relative to the protein-coding gene was consistent with known biology \citep{Tang2020,Baker2012,Mironov2002,Barrangou2007}.

\section{Can RNA homology be rediscovered from known \ac{RNP}s}

A selection of riboswitches and the \ac{CRISPR} repeat element were found to be associated with particular conserved protein domains.
With this direction shown it remains to show that RNA homology can be identified anchoring the search with a conserved protein.
Any of these could presumably be used for validation of the computational discovery pipeline.
The \ac{CRISPR}-Cas system would be interesting to validate; however, in its current state the discovery pipeline extracts only intergenic regions on the same strand as the protein anchor.
The prevalence of the \ac{RNP} and genome availability (limited by Rfam and Entrez entries) were also deciding factors.
As such, the \ac{FMN} riboswitch and its associated protein domain, the RibD C-terminal domain, were used for positive control validation.

\subsection{\ac{FMN} riboswitch as a positive control}
% explain rna -> protein -> rna idea for val

The RibD C-terminal domain, with a profile \ac{HMM} generated in the positive control preparation pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:positive_control_hmm}), was used as an anchor to investigate if the \ac{FMN} riboswitch signal could be rediscovered.
The bacterial family Microbacteriaceae was used as a search space, informed by the phylogenetic distribution of the riboswitch and protein pairing found in the positive control preparation pipeline (Appendix Figure \ref{fig:fmn-itol-tree}).
726 loci were identified in the \emph{Microbacteriaceae} search space (697 genomes), when searched with the curated \ac{FMN} riboswitch model, E-value $< 1\times10^{-5}$. 
Of these 726 loci, 613 were found in the identified anchor-domain flanking regions.
10 out of 13 candidates of the discovery pipline were able to identify (with at least some overlap) all 613 loci.
The outputs of the discovery pipeline were run through the iterative expansion approach described in Section~\ref{sec:iterative}and the resulting alignments were analysed for covariation with R-scape \citep{Rivas2017}.
These final expanded candidates were compared to the true-positive alignment identified with the Rfam curated \ac{FMN} riboswitch model.
Pipeline thresholds for this run are defined in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}.
The resulting candidates are shown in Table~\ref{tab:rscape_summary}, ordered by coordinate overlap identity with the true-positive alignment, along with their covariation analysis F-measures and median summed bit scores against the curated \ac{FMN} riboswitch model.

R-scape F-measure is a harmonic mean of sensitivity and positive predictive value, and is an imperfect measure of the strength of covariation signal in an alignment.
Table \ref{tab:rscape_summary} shows 9 candidates with a mean coordinate overlap of $>67\%$ with the true-positive alignment, and summed median bit scores of $>32$.
This suggests that the candidates identify largely the same genomic loci as the true-positive alignment, and that their probabilistic models describe much of the same structural and sequence information.
The high F-measures of the candidates with high overlap identity and bit scores suggest that the covariance analysis is able to detect the strength of a true conserved signal.

The CaCoFold predicted structures of the two candidates (379 and 531) illustrate the presence of covarying base pairs in the same regions as the true-positive alignment and consensus structure.
As can be seen from the annotated helices, the predicted structures of the candidates are similar to the positive alignment and the Rfam consensus structure, with some variation in the number of helices and their lengths.
Candidates 379 and 531 (figures \ref{fig:cand379-struct} and \ref{fig:cand531-struct}) have predicted structures with many of the same helices as the positive control and Rfam consensus structure.

It should be noted that this is a single, relatively easy positive control.
The \ac{FMN} riboswitch is highly conserved and well characterised, and the protein domain profile used as the anchor was generated from sequences neighbouring the Rfam-annotated riboswitch.
Recovery of this signal is therefore a best-case scenario for the pipeline, rather than a general measure of its sensitivity.

Three of the four negative control domains returned candidates with at least one covarying pair ($E \le 0.05$): SecY (two candidates), the YgaP-like domain (one candidate) and the flagellin domain (nine candidates).
The YadA-like domain returned none.

The highest-scoring negative control candidate was flagellin candidate 327, found in the downstream flanking region, with 11 covarying base pairs and an F-measure of 43.14, which falls within the range of the \ac{FMN} riboswitch candidates (F-measures of 20.00 to 78.95; Table~\ref{tab:rscape_summary}).
A BLASTX search of this candidate, however, gave a significant match to pseudaminic acid synthase (top hit \texttt{WP\_478348184.1} from \emph{Mesorhizobium waitakense}; 85.23\% identity, 100\% query coverage, bit score 449, $E = 6 \times 10^{-157}$; reading frame $-1$).
This indicates that candidate 327 is protein-coding, and that its covariation signal most likely reflects conservation of a protein-coding sequence rather than a structured RNA.
The match was in reading frame $-1$, so the coding sequence lies on the opposite strand to the candidate sequence as extracted.
The downstream flanking region therefore contains an opposite-strand protein-coding gene, a case that the same-strand flank extraction of the pipeline does not exclude.
Flagellin candidates 545 and 589 (also with F-measures above 7.0) returned no significant BLASTX hits.

Apart from candidate 327, the eleven negative control candidates had one to four covarying pairs and F-measures of 1.20 to 8.00, much lower than the \ac{FMN} riboswitch candidates.
These provide an empirical baseline for the covariation signal expected from the pipeline when no known structured RNA is present.
Candidate 327 shows that protein-coding sequence in the flanking regions can produce a signal comparable to that of a real structured RNA, so the check for coding sequence (Section~\ref{subsec:rfam-blastx-check}) is needed before a candidate is interpreted as a structured RNA.

\section{\ac{BLUF} domain \ac{RNP} search}

Having tested the discovery pipeline with positive and negative controls, the pipeline was applied to the \ac{BLUF} domain, which is a light-responsive protein domain.
The \ac{BLUF} domain anchored search was conducted in \emph{Burkholderia}, as this genus contains 701 \ac{BLUF} domain-containing protein sequences \citep{Blum2025}.

Iterative expansion yielded more sequences for both Candidate 130 and 332 (up to 10 and 18 from 6 and 15 respectively); the number of covarying base pairs decreased for Candidate 130 from 4 to none, and increased for Candidate 332 from 1 to 2; similarly, the F-measure increased for Candidate 332 and decreased for Candidate 130 (Table~\ref{tab:bluf_rscape}).

Therefore, the iterative expansion result was used for Candidate 332, while the original alignment was used for Candidate 130 in subsequent analyses.

The R-scape F-measure is an imperfect measure of the strength of covariation signal in an alignment, shown by the positive and negative controls to have some indicative power.
With F-measures of 11.27 and 5.00, respectively, both candidates show only weak evidence of covariation, particularly Candidate 332.
Compared with the negative control candidates (Table~\ref{tab:negative_control_candidates}), the F-measure of Candidate 130 is higher than all but one (the protein-coding flagellin candidate 327), although only moderately above the next highest (8.00), and the F-measure of Candidate 332 lies within the range of the other negative control candidates (1.20 to 8.00).
Another measure of covariance is the number of covarying base pairs.
The 4 covarying base pairs of Candidate 130 (original alignment) and 2 of Candidate 332 ($E \le 0.05$) are within the range of one to four pairs seen for the other negative control candidates, so the number of covarying pairs does not separate them from the negative control baseline.
Overall, Candidate 130 shows marginally stronger covariation than the negative controls, whereas Candidate 332 cannot be distinguished from them.

Neither candidate was found to be a member of any known RNA family.

\section{Covariance model clustering}

Covariance models are the probabilistic models built from multiple sequence alignments to capture the conserved structure and sequences of multiple sequence alignments.
Clustering can be done with pairwise scores, ideally one which is reliable and statistically robust. 
This section describes how three novel scoring approaches were benchmarked against Rfam clans, and how the chosen approach was then applied to cluster the positive control candidates.

Rfam groups families into clans on the basis of shared evolutionary origin, which makes clan membership a useful, if imperfect, ground truth for benchmarking: models in the same clan should recognise each other's sequences, whereas models from different clans should not. 
Rfam clan membership was therefore used to benchmark the pairwise scoring methods.

\subsection{Clustering approaches}

Three approaches were tested: one which searched for a common ancestor with a genetic algorithm, one which used the median bit scores of sequence populations between the models, and one which looked for distributional differences between the models' sequence population bit score distributions. 
The median bit score (empirical p value) clan benchmark test took roughly 11 hours over 64 \ac{CPU} cores, while the optimal sequence and distributional difference approaches took roughly 7 hours over 64 \ac{CPU} cores.
Each was evaluated on its ability to distinguish within-clan pairs from all other pairs (Table~\ref{tab:classifier_comparison}).

\input{tables/cluster_approach_clan_comparisons.tex}

All three approaches gave moderate separation of within-clan pairs from other pairs. 
The similarity ratio and the summed \ac{KS} statistic performed almost identically (\ac{AUC} = 0.832 and 0.835), while the summed median bit score against the null performed less well (\ac{AUC} = 0.767).
Because the \ac{KS} approach provides a per-pair test against a null distribution, which the similarity ratio does not, it was used for all remaining analyses.
The distributions of the common ancestor optimal sequece approach and median bit score approach are shown in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:early-classifiers}.

The distributions for the within-clan pair (SNORA17 and SNORA43) are wider and shifted to higher bit scores than the null distribution of dinucleotide-shuffled sequences.
\ac{KS} tests are a measure of distributional difference, and make no assumptions about the shape of the distributions \citep{Smirnov1939}.
For SNORA17 and SNORA43, which belong to the same clan, the cross-comparison scores were strongly shifted from the null in both directions ($D = 0.673$ and $D = 0.557$; $p = 1.66 \times 10^{-19}$ and $3.22 \times 10^{-30}$). 
For SNORA17 and Pyrobac-1, which belong to different clans, the shift was much weaker in both directions ($D = 0.263$, $p = 5.26 \times 10^{-7}$ and $D = 0.112$, $p = 0.112$).
Summing the two directional statistics therefore gives a single, symmetric measure of mutual recognition.

Across all pairs tested, the summed \ac{KS} statistic was higher for within-clan pairs ($n = 27$) than for other pairs ($n = 919$; Wilcoxon rank-sum $p = 2.76 \times 10^{-9}$, \ac{AUC} = 0.835; Figure~\ref{fig:ks-boxplot}).
Because every model is compared against a single null population it is also considerably cheaper to compute than the bootstrap empirical-p value approach.

The summed \ac{KS} statistic \ac{AUC} of 0.835 (95\% CI: 0.653 -- 0.957) shows moderate seperation of within-clan pairs from other pairs of RNA families.
There are clearly some family pairs, not in clans, which score highly against each other, noteably the micro RNA families (Figure~\ref{fig:ks-boxplot}). 

\subsection{Clustering positive control candidates}

The summed \ac{KS} statistic was used to cluster the postivie control candidate models together with some reference models, using the distance $2 - (D_{A \rightarrow B} + D_{B \rightarrow A})$ and average-linkage hierarchical clustering (Figure~\ref{fig:candidate-clustering}). 
The resulting tree shows candidates clustering into three main groups, one with the consensus FMN riboswitch model clustering with the (\texttt{FMN\_on\_micro}) model and candidates 539, 384, 491, 416, 40, 531, 379, 35 and 647.
These close candidates are also the candidates with the highest coordinate overlap identity to the true-positive alignment (Table~\ref{tab:rscape_summary}).
Candidates 442, 109 and 396 do not cluster with the FMN riboswitch model, and are therefore likely to be distinct from the true-positive alignment.
109 and 396 cluster together, which can also be observed from their predicted secondary structures (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:drh-appendix-structures-2}).
In downstream analysis these candidates could be combined into a single model, potentially improving the alignment and covariation signal.

The (\texttt{FMN\_on\_micro}) model was constructed with the same iterative expansion approach as the candidates, but using the Rfam \ac{FMN} riboswitch model to identify sequences in the same flanking regions as the candidates.
Through this iterative expansion approach the structure of the input model is preserved.
The resulting model is measured to be more similar to the Rfam model than the candidates (Figure~\ref{fig:cluster-dendrogram}), although the consensus model was built from a wider range of sequences.
Seed alignment analysis revealed that similar seed alignment sequences were used to build the candidates and the (\texttt{FMN\_on\_micro}) model (Table~\ref{tab:rscape_summary}).
The closer clustering of the (\texttt{FMN\_on\_micro}) model with the Rfam model than with the candidates may reflect shared construction, since structure is carried through from the Rfam model during iterative expansion.
A control, such as building an \ac{FMN} model de novo from the same sequences, would distinguish these possibilities.
