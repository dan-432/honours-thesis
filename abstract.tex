RNA underpins many of the most transformative advances in modern biotechnology, including RNA interference, CRISPR systems, metabolite-sensing riboswitches, and recently discovered bridge RNA-guided recombination systems. 
Despite their importance, discovering new functional RNAs remains challenging. 
Unlike proteins, RNA sequences often evolve rapidly and are conserved primarily through structure rather than sequence, limiting the effectiveness of conventional homology-search methods. 
Protein homology detection in contrast is an easier solved problem with robust and fast implementations.

Here, we present a protein-first RNA discovery framework that leverages conserved bacterial protein domains to identify candidate cis-regulatory RNAs within neighbouring non-coding regions. 
Minimum free energy folding is unreliable for single sequences and machine learning structure prediction, like AlphaFold, struggle with the small alphabet and the limited training data available. 
Rather than relying solely on structure prediction, we use comparative genomics and identify covarying sites, which mutate together across evolution to preserve base pairing, as evidence of real structure. 
As an initial application, we focus on light-associated protein systems to search for novel light-responsive RNA regulators - looking for potential RNA light switches. 
We further introduce an approach for measuring relatedness between RNA elements, filling a gap in this area. 
Together, this framework provides a targeted and scalable route to RNA discovery, allowing biological pathways of interest to be rapidly screened for previously uncharacterized functional RNA elements.
