\chapter{Introduction}

RNAs play a wide range of roles in cellular processes, many of which have been harnessed for biotechnological applications, and are likely to have many more functions yet to be discovered.
This project aims to identify novel conserved RNA structural elements associated with light-sensing gene families in bacteria.
This introduction provides background and rationale for the screening of novel structured RNA elements in bacterial genomes, and outlines the aims and significance of the project.

\section{Ribonucleic acids}

The central dogma of molecular biology, first articulated by Francis Crick, describes the flow of genetic information: DNA $\rightarrow$ RNA $\rightarrow$ Protein \citep{Crick1970}.
In this classical view, RNA serves primarily as an intermediate messenger, transcribing genetic information from DNA and translating it into proteins at the ribosome.
However, this perspective underrepresents the diversity in the roles of RNA molecules.

In the hypothetical RNA world, RNA molecules are thought to have performed many of the roles now largely carried out by proteins and DNA, including catalysis, regulation, and information storage \citep{Kruger1982, Guerrier-Takada1983, Gilbert1986, Breaker2012}.
Although these functions have been largely superseded by proteins and DNA, numerous RNA-based regulatory mechanisms persist in modern organisms.

\subsection{Functional RNAs in biology and biotechnology}
% needs a rewrite, this is just pasted together for now

The diverse functional roles of RNA are driven by their structural and reactive diversity. 
Naturally occurring functional RNAs include purely RNA systems, riboswitches and ribozymes, and those in complexes with proteins, while \emph{in vitro} selected RNAs include ﬂuorogens and aptazymes \citep{Micura2020}.

Ribozymes are catalytic RNAs that accelerate chemical reactions, much like protein enzymes.
Beyond the ribosome and spliceosome, several small self-cleaving ribozymes exist in nature, including the hammerhead, hairpin, \ac{HDV}, glmS, twister, and pistol ribozymes \citep{Roth2014, Weinberg2015, Micura2020}. 

RNA has been central to several major biotechnological breakthroughs. 
\ac{CRISPR}-Cas9 gene editing harnesses guide RNAs for programmable genome modification \citep{Jinek2012}. 
\ac{mRNA} vaccines against \ac{COVID-19} demonstrated the therapeutic potential of synthetic \acp{mRNA} \citep{Leong2025}. 
\ac{RNAi} enables targeted gene silencing in research and therapeutics \citep{Fire1998}.

RNA aptamers such as Spinach bind fluorogenic compounds and fluoresce upon binding, enabling RNA visualization in living cells \citep{Paige2011}.
The photoriboswitch Werewolf-1, generated through \emph{in vitro} selection, responds to light by binding photoisomerizable ligands, offering potential for optogenetic control of gene expression \citep{Rotstan2020}.
These RNAs, involved in fluorescence and photoreception, have broad relevance for medical research, biotechnology, and agricultural applications \citep{Micura2020}.

\subsection{Ribonucleoprotein complexes}

While some RNAs function independently, many operate in \ac{RNP} complexes.
The ribosome is one such \ac{RNP} with critical function in all cells.
This large complex catalyses peptide bond formation, with \ac{rRNA} forming the catalytic core, while ribosomal proteins provide structural support and regulatory functionality \citep{Wahl2009}.
Spliceosomes are another example of essential \acp{RNP} in eukaryotes \citep{Wahl2009}.

In bacteria, several classes of \acp{RNP} mediate post-transcriptional regulation. 
\Acp{sRNA} often require the RNA chaperone Hfq to facilitate base pairing with target mRNAs, thereby modulating transcript stability \citep{Vogel2006}.

\ac{CRISPR}-Cas systems exemplify the complexity of bacterial \acp{RNP}.
\Acp{crRNA} guide Cas nucleases to complementary DNA or RNA targets, enabling sequence-specific nucleic acid cleavage \citep{Barrangou2007}.
This mechanism has famously been repurposed as a gene-editing tool \citep{Jinek2012}.

\citet{Durrant2024} recently discovered Bridge RNA, which forms a complex with a transposase.
This system was found in a family of minimal insertion sequences, IS110, which expresses a transposase that is essential for the mobility of the insertion sequence.
Prior to experimental validation, the discovery was done by a computational screen for transposase homology and subsequent computational identification of conserved RNA structured elements directly flanking the transposase genes.
Bridge RNA systems are further shown to be reprogrammable to offer targeted control of transposase activity, enabling precise genome editing through site-specific insertion, excision and inversion.
This is a major finding in the field of gene editing, adding to the library of tools available for precise genome manipulation.

\subsection{Cis-regulatory RNAs}

%Including light sensitive/related, give some importance of these

Cis-regulatory RNAs are structured RNA elements that regulate the expression of genes located on the same transcript.
Riboswitches are a well-studied class of cis-regulatory RNAs that modulate gene expression in response to some stimuli, often metabolites \citep{Breaker2023}.
Although seemingly less abundant than protein factors that perform similar functions, riboswitches are recognized as important regulatory elements across all domains of life.
They are typically embedded within messenger RNAs and control gene expression through ligand-induced conformational changes.

Transcriptional riboswitches control formation of Rho-independent terminators — intrinsic termination structures consisting of a hairpin followed by a uridine-rich tract. 
Depending on the riboswitch, ligand binding may promote terminator formation or stabilize an antiterminator structure, enabling transcriptional read-through.
Similarly, translational riboswitches can sequester or expose the ribosome-binding site in response to ligand binding \citep{Breaker2023}.

\subsection{RNA structure determines function}

RNA molecules, much like proteins, fold into complex structures that enable diverse functions \citep{Wang2021}.
They typically exist as single-stranded molecules, which can fold back on themselves through intramolecular base pairing to form secondary and tertiary structures.
RNA secondary structure arises from Watson-Crick base pairing (A-U and G-C) and wobble pairing between nucleotides within the same strand \citep{Tinoco1999}. 
Figure ~\ref{fig:rna-structure} illustrates the diversity of RNA secondary structure elements, which enable a variety of functions, enabling interactions with proteins, other RNAs, and small molecules \citep{Marklund2023}.
Common secondary structures include hairpins, consisting of a base-paired stem capped by an unpaired loop; internal loops and bulges, made up of unpaired nucleotides within a helical stem; junctions where three or more helical stems converge; and pseudoknots, where nucleotides in a loop pair with regions outside that loop \citep{Staple2005}.

Beyond secondary structure, RNAs fold into more complex tertiary structures through long range interactions \citep{Batey1999}.
These include base triplets, ribose zippers, A-minor motifs, and tetraloop-receptor interactions, which stabilize compact RNA folds.

Although there are many examples of structured functional RNAs, it is important to note there are many cases where sequence alone provides function \citep{Fire1998, Backofen2014}.
The relationship between RNA structure and function is exemplified by riboswitches, a class of regulatory RNAs.
The aptamer domain folds into a specific three-dimensional pocket that selectively binds a target ligand through a combination of electrostatic interactions and shape complementarity \citep{Kavita2023}.
Ligand binding stabilizes one conformational state over another, thereby modulating transcription termination or initiation.
This allosteric mechanism, whereby binding at an aptamer site affects a distal functional region, demonstrates how RNA structure enables regulatory function.

\section{Current approaches to functional RNA discovery}

It is predicted that a large number of functional RNAs remain undiscovered, potentially on the order of thousands \citep{Kavita2023}.
Breaker and colleagues have pioneered systematic riboswitch and ribozyme discovery, identifying over 55 distinct riboswitch classes as of 2022 \citep{Breaker2022}. % drop this or find up to date number of riboswitches discovered.
Other major RNA-related discoveries, such as ribosomes, \ac{CRISPR}-Cas systems, and Bridge RNA, also occurred through exploratory research approaches \citep{Bruck2025}.

\subsection{Computational discovery approaches}
% move some of this to the end of the intro perhaps when leading up to methods

Bioinformatic sequence-based searches using tools like \ac{BLAST} identify homologous sequences, but many functional RNAs have divergent sequences detectable only through structural conservation.
Structure based searches have proven successful, like the bioinformatic search for twister ribozymes by \citet{Roth2014} discovering roughly 2,700 ribozyme candidates.
These techniques search on sequence and secondary structure patterns, but require existing alignments and structure models, limiting their utility for discovering entirely novel RNA classes.

RNA structure prediction remains a challenging problem, particularly when given a single sequence and no homology structures are known, as would be the case with a genome wide search for novel RNA structures in a single species \citep{Backofen2014}.
Comparative approaches using multiple homologous sequences provide greater predictive power for RNA structure inference \citep{Kagaya2026}.
Figure ~\ref{fig:covariation} illustrates the idea of covariation and how it can be used to identify conserved RNA structures across related species.
Where pairs of nucleotides covary across species, i.e., they mutate together to preserve base pairing, this provides evidence of conserved RNA structure.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/introduction/covariationfig.pdf}
    \caption[Identification of covarying sites in RNA alignments]{\textbf{Identification of covarying sites in RNA alignments.} 
    Covarying sites are pairs of nucleotides that mutate together across evolution to preserve base pairing, providing evidence of conserved RNA structure.
    In this example, a C-G base pair in one species is replaced by an A-U base pair in another species, maintaining the overall secondary structure of the RNA.
    Covariation analysis is a powerful tool for identifying conserved RNA structures across related species, enabling the discovery of conserved structural features. }
    \label{fig:covariation}
\end{figure}

The CMFinder tool \citet{Yao2006}, investigates covariance across multiple sequences, enabling the identification of conserved structural features.
These conserved regions show evidence of selective constraint on structure, indicating potential functional importance. 
Such RNA elements may represent riboswitches, ribozymes, or other classes of functional RNAs.
The Weinberg lab has had several notable successes with their tool, CMfinder, \citep{Baker2012,Roth2014,Weinberg2015,Baker2012}.
\citet{Roth2014} also employed CMfinder to identify a new class of self cleaving ribozymes, the twister ribozyme, which has since been found to be widespread in nature.
In that paper hammerhead and twister ribozymes were observed to commonly reside near each other and to certain protein coding genes, although noting it still a biological mystery. 
\citet{Weinberg2015} followed up on this observation with a screening of sequences nearby known ribozyme and ribozyme associated protein domain coding regions.
This approach successfully identified additional ribozyme classes including pistol and hatchet.
The 2025 paper, \citet{Sack2025}, recently identified 16 new structured RNA classes in plants using a CMfinder-based bioinformatic screen.
This study focused on structured RNAs involved in alternative splicing.

\subsection{Experimental approaches to discovery and generation of functional RNAs}

Transcriptome mapping approaches have proven successful for unbiased riboswitch discovery. 
Term-seq maps RNA 3' termini genome-wide to identify premature transcription termination sites characteristic of riboswitches and attenuators \citep{Dar2016}. 
This approach has uncovered numerous antibiotic-responsive riboswitches controlling resistance genes in bacterial pathogens and within the human microbiome.
RNA structure probing methods characterize secondary structures, tertiary interactions, and dynamic conformational changes. 
\ac{SHAPE-seq} chemically modifies unpaired nucleotides, enabling genome-wide structure mapping \citep{Lucks2011}. 
This approach can identify structured regions throughout transcriptomes, revealing RNA switches, ribozymes, and protein-binding sites.
\ac{DMS-MaPseq} extends this approach to structural probing \emph{in vivo} \citep{Zubradt2017}.
A variety of methods exist for studying protein-RNA interactions from both protein- and RNA-centric approaches \citep{Yi2025}.
While powerful for validation and mechanistic characterization, computational approaches have been successful in extending the scope of RNA research \citep{Backofen2014}.

\section{The Twilight Zone of RNA and protein homology}

Homology is being used here as a catch all term for sequence and structural similarity due to shared ancestry.
Orthology, parology and xenology are all included in this broad definition of homology.
Othologs are are genes which have diverged through speciation events \citep{Jensen2001}.
Conversly, paralogs are genes that have diverged through duplication events within a species \citep{Jensen2001}.
Xenology is a term used to describe genes that have been transferred between species through horizontal gene transfer \citep{Jensen2001}.
Homoloy is often infered from sequences or structures share more similarity than would be expected by chance \citep{Pearson2013}.
Homolgous genes have shared ancestry and potentially shared function and evolutionary pressure, but the processes that have led to their divergence can make it difficult to detect homology through sequence similarity alone.

The Twilight Zone of homology detection refers to the range of sequence similarity where it is difficult to confidently infer homology based on sequence alone \citep{Rost1999}.
The Twilight Zone of protein homology was estimated by \citet{Rost1999} at 20-35\% sequence identity, where the probability of correctly identifying homologs drops significantly.
This means that even if two homologous proteins can be identified as such until they have diverged to the point where their sequences are less than 20-35\% identical.

RNA homology detection is one way of identifying functional RNAs, as conservation across homology can detect similar selective pressures and functionality.
Further, with homologous sequences found across multiple species, comparative approaches can be used to identify conserved RNA structures \citep{Backofen2014}.
RNA homology searches are complicated by the fact that RNA structure is often more conserved than sequence .
The Twilight Zone of RNA homology detection is less well defined than for proteins, but was estimated by \citet{McGimpsey2019} to be around 35-50\% sequence identity.
This means it is more difficult to detect deep homology in RNA sequences than in protein sequences.

\section{A protein-first approach to RNA discovery}
% introcuce this more as a protein first approach rather than gene family

The approach being proposed here is a protein-first approach to RNA discovery, leveraging the relative ease of protein homology detection to identify conserved protein domains and deeper evolutionary relationships, and then searching for conserved RNA structures in neighbouring non-coding regions (Figure~\ref{fig:protein-vs-rna-first-pipeline}).
Beyond the successes of gene family-targeted searches with CMfinder, there is precedent for riboswitches being encoded upstream of certain gene families \citep{Micura2020, Kavita2023}.
This approach also provides a less computationally intensive alternative to genome-wide screens, allowing for wider searches across more species.
It harnesses the power of evolutionary conservation and leverages the biological assumption that functionally related genes (i.e. with domain homology) may share regulatory mechanisms.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/introduction/proteinfirst.pdf}
    \caption[A protein-first approach to RNA discovery]{\textbf{A protein-first approach to RNA discovery.} 
    Typical RNA discovery approaches are RNA-first, searching for conserved RNA structures across genomes and then associating them with nearby genes.
    In contrast, a protein-first approach leverages the relative ease of protein homology detection to identify conserved protein domains and deeper evolutionary relationships and then searches for conserved RNA structures in neighbouring non-coding regions.}
    \label{fig:protein-vs-rna-first-pipeline}
\end{figure}

Because of the potential importance of light-sensing systems in biotechnology, this project screened for conserved RNA structures located near light-sensing gene families.
Light-sensing systems in bacteria often involve complex regulatory networks, and RNA-based mechanisms have been shown to play roles in light acclimation responses in cyanobacteria \citep{Hihara2015}.

A recently published study by a group in Copenhagen, \citet{Geissler2026}, describes a bioinformatic screen similar to the one undertaken here.
In that work 931 orthologous cyanobacteria genes involved in various pathways were targeted for nearby conserved RNA structure discovery using CMfinder.
Approximately 400 novel conserved RNA structures were identified, some of which were in intergenic regions up and downstream of light-sensing and photosynthetic genes.
This work demonstrates the potential for searching for conserved RNA structures near light-sensing gene families, and the potential for discovering novel regulatory elements in these systems.
The approach taken in that study was, however, more of an RNA-first discovery pipeline where large sets of non-coding regions from a relatively small number of genomes (202) were screened for conserved RNA structures.
Filtering for GC content and other non-structural features was used to reduce the number of candidate structures.

With a protein-first approach, the search spaces can be expanded to include a much larger number of genomes, and the search can be targeted to specific protein domains of interest.
With a larger search space and a more targeted approach, the power of a covariance analysis is expected to increase, as power depends on the number and phylogenetic diversity of the sequences analysed.
More diverse sequences give more opportunities to observe covarying positions, which should improve the statistical significance of the results and the chance of detecting biologically relevant signals.

\subsection{A search target: Blue Light sensor Using \ac{FAD}}

The \ac{BLUF} domain is a light-sensing protein domain that binds flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and undergoes a conformational change upon blue light absorption \citep{Kita2005,Gomelsky2002}.
This conformational change can modulate the activity of associated effector domains, enabling light-dependent regulation.
\ac{BLUF} was chosen as a proof-of-concept search target for its potential to be involved in an \ac{RNP} 'light switch' complex.

The domain is composed of a five-stranded $\beta$-sheet flanked by two $\alpha$-helices, forming a compact structure that binds FAD.
Three conserved residues, Asn31, Asn32 and Gln50, are thought to be critical for flavin binding and light-induced conformational changes \citep{Kita2005}.

\subsection{Bacterial genomes as a search space}

Riboswitches have been most extensively discovered and experimentally validated in bacteria and seem to rarely have homologs in eukaryotes \citep{Kavita2023}.
This makes bacteria a reasonable domain for riboswitch discovery efforts.

Beyond riboswitches, many other functional RNAs in bacteria act through protein partners.
Small RNAs and RNA-binding proteins such as Hfq and CsrA form extensive post-transcriptional regulatory networks \citep{Romeo1998, Vogel2006, Holmqvist2018}, and the RNA component of ribonuclease P is a catalytic RNA-protein complex conserved across bacteria \citep{Guerrier-Takada1983}.
CRISPR-Cas systems, in which guide RNAs direct protein effectors to their targets, are also widespread in bacterial genomes \citep{Barrangou2007, Jinek2012}, and new RNA-guided systems continue to be described \citep{Bruck2025}.
This provides rationale for searching for conserved RNA structured elements in bacterial genomes.

Extensive phylogenetic diversity is available among sequenced bacterial genomes.
As of April 2026, over 870,000 bacterial genomes spanning 180,000 bacterial species clusters have been sequenced and organised in databases such as \ac{GTDB} \citep{GTDB2026}.
This represents vast evolutionary distances, bacteria diverged over 3 billion years ago, providing the deep phylogenies necessary for detecting conserved structures through covariation analysis.

Though bacterial regulation is not simple \citep{Backofen2014, Breaker2018, Gottesman2011}, unlike eukaryotes, bacterial mRNAs typically lack capping, splicing, and nuclear export \citep{Moore2009, Wahl2009}, and transcription and translation are often coupled \citep{Kohler2017, Irastortza-Olaziregi2021}.
This may facilitate the identification of conserved RNA elements from genomic DNA sequences, as fewer processing steps (e.g. splicing) complicate the relationship between genomic sequence and mature transcript.

\subsection{Bioinformatic approach}

The right hand path of Figure ~\ref{fig:protein-vs-rna-first-pipeline} illustrates the protein-first approach to RNA discovery taken in this project.
The general approach is to identify conserved protein domains, and then search for conserved RNA structures in neighbouring non-coding regions.
The first step is to identify conserved protein domains of interest, such as \ac{BLUF}, across a wide range of bacterial genomes.
Tools such as FASTA and BLAST can be used to identify homologous proteins by comparing sequences, while HMMER uses both sequence and structure information from probabilistic models to identify homologous protein domains \citep{Pearson2013}.
\citet{Durrant2024} had success using HMMER in their protein-first approach, and this approach will be used here as well.
Once these domains are identified, the genomic regions flanking these genes can be extracted and analyzed for conserved RNA structures using comparative genomics approaches.
These include tools such as CMfinder, which identifies conserved RNA secondary structures using a comparative genomics approach \citep{Yao2006}.
Alternatively, clustering and alignment tools (MAFTT and MMseqs2 \citep{Kallenborn2025, Katoh2013}) can be used to identify local sequence homology before searching for conserved RNA structures in these regions by covariance analysis.
Several tools exist for covariance analysis, including R-scape, which uses statistical models to identify covarying base pairs (Figure ~\ref{fig:covariation}) in RNA alignments \citep{Rivas2017}.
This second apprach was used in this project after initial attempts to use CMfinder were unsuccessful, and is described in more detail in the methods section.

Probabilistic models of RNA structure and sequence can be built from identified structural element candidates, most commonly these are \ac{CM}s.
\ac{CM}s are statistical models that capture both the sequence and structural features of RNA families, enabling sensitive searches for homologous sequences in genomic databases \citep{Nawrocki2013}.

\section{Project aims and hypothesis}

The overarching aim is to discover novel structured RNA elements associated with bacterial light-sensing systems by leveraging comparative genomics. 
Gene-family-targeted approaches offer a computationally tractable approach for identifying regulatory elements linked to specific biological pathways. 
Light-sensing gene families are of particular interest due to their importance in environmental adaptation and their potential relevance to biotechnological applications. 
Identifying conserved RNA structures associated with these genes may reveal previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanisms.
The first aim of this project is therefore to screen for conserved RNA structural elements located near the \ac{BLUF} domain across diverse bacterial genomes. 
By examining intergenic and transcript regions associated with these genes, the project aims to predict conserved RNA secondary structures using comparative genomics approaches that identify covariation patterns indicative of selective constraint.
The second aim is to develop a protein-first structured RNA discovery pipeline, capable of systematically screening for conserved RNA structures. 
This pipeline is intended to be reusable and adaptable for future studies, targeting additional gene families or biological pathways. 
Together, these aims will contribute both new candidate structured RNA elements and a methodological platform for search for structured RNAs in nature.

Further, this work aims to fill a gap in the field for a reliable statistical method for measuring relatedness between RNA elements, which is important for understanding the evolutionary relationships and structural similarities among RNA motifs.
This has remained a challenge in the field despite the development of several methods for RNA structured element comparison \citep{Pratiwi2022,Eggenhofer2013}.

The central hypothesis of this work is that protein homology can be leveraged in a discovery pipeline for identifying RNA homology in \ac{RNP} systems.
This will be tested by recovery of a known structured RNA element.
This relies on bioinformatic tools being able to identify conserved protein domains and then search for conserved RNA structures in neighbouring non-coding regions.
Testing if any identified conserved RNA structures have any function or are involved in light sensing is beyond the scope of this project, but the discovery of novel conserved RNA structures will provide a foundation for future experimental validation and functional characterization.
